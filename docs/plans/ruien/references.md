# Ruien: reference material (gathered 2026-10-18)

Short facts with sources, for designing the Ruien as a playable network in autumn 1873.
No images are downloaded. Image links go to their Wikimedia Commons file page (or a museum page) with the licence shown there.
Where sources disagree or a fact was not found, this file says so. Treat travel blogs and guide quotes as softer than archives.

## 1. Antwerp Ruien

### Network and history

- About 8 km of canals, vlieten and moats. Antwerp was called "Ruienstad" around 1250. ([ruien.be](https://en.ruien.be/))
- Sluices (schuiven) formed a flushing system from the 14th century. They were shut at high tide and opened at low tide,
  so the water carried waste to the Schelde. It never kept the ruien clean. ([Antwerp city magazine, Open Monumentendag 2024](https://magazine.antwerpen.be/open-monumentendag-2024/de-ruien))
- From the 16th century owners could vault the rui on their own land at their own cost. In the 17th century the city paid premiums.
  The result is a mosaic of vaults. ([city magazine](https://magazine.antwerpen.be/open-monumentendag-2024/de-ruien))
- Early 19th century: only the Meir, Jezuietenrui, Minderbroedersrui and part of the Suikerrui were covered.
  By 1835 about 75% was covered. The Brouwersvliet was the last, covered in 1882. ([city magazine](https://magazine.antwerpen.be/open-monumentendag-2024/de-ruien))
  One blog says enclosure ran "to 1885". ([Go Eat Do 2013](https://go-eat-do.com/2013/12/ruien-antwerp/))
- A Dutch source says the city decided in 1823 to vault the ruien after epidemics and stench. ([dagjeweg.nl](https://www.dagjeweg.nl/tip/4135/Ruienwandeling))
- In the 19th century the ruien were vaulted and their walls straightened. By the end of the century they were officially sewers.
  ([HMdb marker, Suikerrui](https://www.hmdb.org/m.asp?m=224668))
- The 1866 cholera epidemic killed 2,961 people in Antwerp. ([Go Eat Do 2013](https://go-eat-do.com/2013/12/ruien-antwerp/))
- The ruien are still connected to the tidal Schelde. Tours do not run at high water, spring tide or heavy rain.
  ([nl.wikipedia: Antwerpse ruien](https://nl.wikipedia.org/wiki/Antwerpse_ruien))

### Vaults, sizes, materials

- About 200 vault types. The largest about 9 m wide and 6 m high; the smallest about 3 m wide and 2 m high.
  This is a guide's figure, not a survey. ([Go Eat Do 2013](https://go-eat-do.com/2013/12/ruien-antwerp/))
- Vault shapes named by the city: barrel vaults (tonronde welfsels), basket arches (korfbogen) and pointed arches (spitsbogen).
  ([city magazine](https://magazine.antwerpen.be/open-monumentendag-2024/de-ruien))
- Materials: brick, sandstone and bluestone (hardsteen). The stone used "reflected people's wealth". ([Go Eat Do 2013](https://go-eat-do.com/2013/12/ruien-antwerp/))
- The "kathedraal": a part of the ruien at Sint-Paulusplaats so wide and high it recalls a cathedral. A banquet for English guests
  was held on an underground square there in the late 19th century; Allied parties met there in the Second World War.
  This is the real model for a large hall. No size given. ([nl.wikipedia](https://nl.wikipedia.org/wiki/Antwerpse_ruien))
- Boom brick: the Rupel area around Boom was Belgium's main brick region. Hoffmann kilns came in from about 1870.
  No source found that names Boom brick in the ruien vaults; it is a likely guess for 19th-century work only.
  ([ERIH: Boom brickmaking](https://www.erih.net/i-want-to-go-there/site/ecomuseum-and-archives-of-the-boom-brickmaking-region))

### Floor, water and air

- Each tunnel has a drainage channel 15 to 50 cm deep. Rain can raise the water by up to 30 cm. ([Go Eat Do 2013](https://go-eat-do.com/2013/12/ruien-antwerp/))
- The first 300 m of the tour is by boat, where the water is deepest. That stretch is kept flooded on purpose to show
  what a rui looked like before sewage and rain water were split. The walk then runs about 1.6 km. ([nl.wikipedia](https://nl.wikipedia.org/wiki/Antwerpse_ruien))
- Today most sewage runs in pipes fixed inside the ruien. Only in heavy rain does waste water run through the ruien again.
  ([nl.wikipedia](https://nl.wikipedia.org/wiki/Antwerpse_ruien))
- A "huge chimney" brings fresh air down. ([Go Eat Do 2013](https://go-eat-do.com/2013/12/ruien-antwerp/))

### What a visitor sees today

- Vaults, foundations, bridges and locks; walking and a boat ride. ([HMdb marker](https://www.hmdb.org/m.asp?m=224668))
- Mud and slime underfoot, a mud channel, drips from the vaults, rushing water heard ahead. Smell of "sea air, pig manure and mud".
  ([VICE NL, 2011](https://www.vice.com/nl/article/19024-revision/))
- Rats: "Sometimes we see one, sometimes a hundred." Black and brown (and tropical) spiders with white nests.
  ([Go Eat Do 2013](https://go-eat-do.com/2013/12/ruien-antwerp/), [VICE NL](https://www.vice.com/nl/article/19024-revision/))
- Lighting: hand torches; some modern green and blue lights. Visitors wear overalls and boots.
  ([Go Eat Do 2013](https://go-eat-do.com/2013/12/ruien-antwerp/), [ruien.be](https://en.ruien.be/))
- Ceramic street-name plates on the walls, after the Paris model, so you know which street is overhead.
  ([nl.wikipedia](https://nl.wikipedia.org/wiki/Antwerpse_ruien))
- Lime deposits: no source found. Expect them on brick and mortar in any wet vault, but this is not sourced.

### Ruihuis

- Suikerrui 21, near the Grote Markt. Tours start here and end at the Stadsmagazijn, Keistraat. Public since 2005.
  Boat trips also ran in 1964 and 1965. ([nl.wikipedia](https://nl.wikipedia.org/wiki/Antwerpse_ruien), [Go Eat Do 2013](https://go-eat-do.com/2013/12/ruien-antwerp/))

## 2. Comparable systems, 1840s to 1880s

### Paris (Belgrand, from 1854)

- Belgrand ran the water service from 1854, and water and sewers from 1867 to 1878. Every street got a gallery. ([fr.wikipedia: Eugene Belgrand](https://fr.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eug%C3%A8ne_Belgrand))
- The Asnieres collector (Concorde to Clichy): 4.4 m high, 5.6 m wide. Called the widest in the world then. ([fr.wikipedia: Belgrand](https://fr.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eug%C3%A8ne_Belgrand))
- Gallery classes ([fr.wikipedia: Egouts de Paris](https://fr.wikipedia.org/wiki/%C3%89gouts_de_Paris)):
  - main collectors: 5 to 6 m wide, central channel (cunette) 3.5 m wide, usually under boulevards;
  - secondary collectors: 3 m wide, channel 1.2 m;
  - outfalls (emissaires): round, 2.5 to 6 m across, not walkable.
- From 1820 sewers changed from cut stone with a flat base to an egg (ovoid) section in millstone grit and cement, tall enough to stand in. ([fr.wikipedia](https://fr.wikipedia.org/wiki/%C3%89gouts_de_Paris))
- Side walkways (banquettes) line the channel; the cleaning wagons run along them. ([fr.wikipedia](https://fr.wikipedia.org/wiki/%C3%89gouts_de_Paris))
- Network: 143 km in 1853. Sewermen: 276 in 1865, 627 in 1873, 900 in 1885. ([fr.wikipedia](https://fr.wikipedia.org/wiki/%C3%89gouts_de_Paris))
- Public visits began for the 1867 World Fair, by wagon and boat. In 1892 visitors rode a wagon pushed by four sewermen in white.
  ([showcaves.com](https://showcaves.com/english/fr/subterranea/ParisEgouts.html), [pariszigzag](https://www.pariszigzag.fr/sortir-paris/balade-paris/visite-des-egouts-de-paris/))
- Museum: [Musee des egouts de Paris](https://www.paris.fr/lieux/musee-des-egouts-de-paris-5059), near Pont de l'Alma.

### London (Bazalgette, 1859 to 1875)

- Intercepting sewers built 1859 to 1865. Bazalgette's article: 82 miles (132 km) of main intercepting sewers and 1,100 miles of street sewers.
  The system article: six interceptors of almost 100 miles, fed by 450 miles of main sewers. 318 million bricks; Portland cement.
  ([en.wikipedia: Joseph Bazalgette](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Joseph_Bazalgette), [London sewer system](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/London_sewer_system))
- Egg-shaped brick tunnels, chosen over narrow pipes. ([Science Museum Group](https://collection.sciencemuseumgroup.org.uk/people/cp110961))
- Sizes from 1861: the high-level interceptor starts round at about 4 ft (1.2 m) and grows to at least 10.5 ft (3.2 m).
  The southern outfall tunnel is about 11.5 ft (3.5 m) across and 7.5 miles long. The north low-level sewer runs about 37 ft below the high and middle levels.
  Storm overflow chambers at the ends; storm overflow branches to the Thames at Vauxhall and Deptford. ([All the Year Round, vol. V, 1861, p. 488](https://djo.org.uk/all-the-year-round/volume-v/page-488.html))
- King's Scholars' Pond Sewer: egg shape, 5.5 ft x 3.5 ft (1.7 x 1.1 m). ([sub-urban.com](https://www.sub-urban.com/category/anatomy-of-a-junction))
- Crossness (opened 4 April 1865): four beam engines lift sewage 30 to 40 ft (9 to 12 m). The covered reservoir is 6.5 acres, 17 ft deep,
  about 27 million gallons, under a brick vaulted roof. It emptied on the ebb tide; penstocks then closed the outlet culverts.
  ([en.wikipedia: Crossness](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Crossness_Pumping_Station), [London Gardens Trust](https://londongardenstrust.org/conservation/inventory/site-record?ID=BEX011a), [AIM25 record](https://atom.aim25.com/index.php/southern-outfall-works-x-crossness-pumping-station-2))
  This tidal storage chamber is the closest real model for a brick flood-relief hall with piers. Pier spacing not found.
- Abbey Mills (1865 to 1868): eight beam engines on a cross plan; lift about 40 ft (12 m) into the Northern Outfall Sewer.
  ([en.wikipedia: Abbey Mills](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Abbey_Mills_Pumping_Station), [Northern Outfall Sewer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Northern_Outfall_Sewer))
- System history from the operator: [Thames Water](https://www.thameswater.co.uk/about-us/community/days-out/riverside-spaces/history-of-london-sewer-system).

### Brussels (covering of the Senne, 1867 to 1871)

- Work began 13 February 1867; opened 30 November 1871 when the council opened the rebuilt sluice gates.
- The covered section is 2.2 km long. It is two parallel brick tunnels, each 6 m wide, plus two side drains, one per bank.
  It lies under a straight boulevard 30 m wide, in a Y shape. Wells and pumps held the ground water down during the work.
- After a small flood in 1873, the 1876 rains brought 85 m3/s through without trouble. The tunnels later became the premetro (1976).
  ([en.wikipedia: Covering of the Senne](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Covering_of_the_Senne), [fr.wikipedia](https://fr.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vo%C3%BBtement_de_la_Senne))

### Hamburg (Lindley, after the 1842 fire)

- The first modern underground sewers on the European mainland. Brick and walkable. Only 1 m of fall over 3 km.
  Large trunk sewers (Stammsiele) run below the smaller ones. Backflow flaps (Rueckstauklappen) guard against high water,
  with emergency outlets into the canals (Fleete). 11 km built in three years.
  ([de.wikipedia: William Lindley](https://de.wikipedia.org/wiki/William_Lindley))
- A monument to Lindley stands at the entrance to the Kuhmuehlen trunk sewer near Baumwall. ([de.wikipedia](https://de.wikipedia.org/wiki/William_Lindley))
- Image: [File:PPN612289664 Siel-System (1864).jpg](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:PPN612289664_Siel-System_(1864).jpg): an 1864 sewer-system sheet. Public domain.

## 3. Period props and dressing

Main period source: Baldwin Latham, *Sanitary Engineering*, 2nd ed., London 1878 (public domain).
[Commons file page](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Sanitary_engineering_-_a_guide_to_the_construction_of_works_of_sewerage_and_house_drainage,_with_tables_for_facilitating_the_calculations_of_the_engineer_(IA_sanitaryengineer00lath).pdf),
[archive.org](https://archive.org/details/sanitaryengineer00lath). Plate XVII shows tide valves; Plate XVIII shows penstocks.

### Tide valves and flap doors (vloeddeuren, klepdeuren)

- An outfall below high-water mark must have a tidal valve to keep the river out. (Latham, p. 457)
- Two kinds: tankard valves (hinged at the top like a tankard lid) and float valves. Made of wood, wrought iron or cast iron.
  Cast iron is usual for sewers. Wooden ones of elm or oak, sometimes built like a lock gate, are used in the Fens. (Latham, p. 458)
- Latham's balanced flap hangs from the top, with a weighted spindle so it can be set to just stay open; the smallest back current shuts it. (Latham, p. 375)
- Hamburg used backflow flaps on its 1840s sewers. ([de.wikipedia](https://de.wikipedia.org/wiki/William_Lindley))

### Penstocks and sluice gates with winding gear

- Penstocks are sluices in a sewer to control or stop the flow, or to pen up water for flushing.
  At a tidal outfall a penstock should always back up the tide valve. (Latham, p. 461)
- Usually cast-iron frames and doors. Raised by rack and worm wheel (best) or by chains over drums (jerky).
  Faces planed or lined with gun-metal; wedges on the door press it tight when lowered. (Latham, p. 461)
- Large doors have counterweights and compound gearing: a man puts a key on the slow spindle to start the door,
  then moves the key to the fast spindle. Bearings bushed with gun-metal against rust. (Latham, p. 462)
- Crossness closed its reservoir outlets with penstocks as the ebb turned. ([London Gardens Trust](https://londongardenstrust.org/conservation/inventory/site-record?ID=BEX011a))
- The Antwerp schuiven worked on the same tide rhythm (see section 1). Their mechanism was not found.

### Manholes, lampholes, covers, step irons

- Manholes are brick-lined (steined) wells down to the sewer, over the crown or beside it.
  London also used side entrances from the footpath, so spoil could be lifted without blocking traffic. (Latham, pp. 418 to 419)
- A manhole cover opening is not more than about 2 ft (0.6 m) across. Some covers take a safety grating for ventilation. (Latham, pp. 419 to 421)
- Lampholes are small shafts to hang a lamp down, so a man at the next manhole can see along the sewer. (Latham, p. 418)
- Step irons: not described in the sources found. Latham's figures of manholes may show them; check Plate XIII and Figs. 121 to 122.
- Cast-iron covers today weigh about 45 to 135 kg. ([en.wikipedia: Manhole cover](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Manhole_cover))
- Ladders: the 1861 Woolwich works had shafts "like the mouths of huge mines with ladders going down at different inclinations". ([All the Year Round, 1861](https://djo.org.uk/all-the-year-round/volume-v/page-488.html))
- Images: [Category:Manhole covers in London](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/Category:Manhole_covers_in_London) (mixed licences, mostly CC BY-SA);
  [File:Fleet sewer hatch.jpg](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Fleet_sewer_hatch.jpg) (CC BY 2.0).

### Light

- Sewer gas (marsh gas, much of it from leaking gas pipes) explodes with air, so "it is unsafe to enter sewers with naked lights".
  Latham says sewers should be lit with safety lamps when men go in. (Latham, p. 324)
- No source found for fixed gas lighting inside working sewers in the 1870s. Workers carried lamps; tours today use torches.
- Nadar photographed the Paris sewers in 1861 with artificial light; his pictures show the period look. (see Paris images)
- The Webb sewer gas lamp (street lamp burning sewer gas) is from the 1890s; too late for 1873.
  ([historic-uk.com](https://www.historic-uk.com/HistoryMagazine/DestinationsUK/Farting-Lane/))

### Workers, tools and boats

- Paris, 19th century: the main tool was the rabot, a 2 m pole with an iron blade at a right angle.
  Men set wooden boards across the sewer, then lifted them to flush the debris with the rush. Sand from roads and building sites
  mixed with organic matter and choked the sewers; it was shovelled out and hoisted in buckets. Teams of 5 to 8 men per district.
  ([fr.wikipedia: Egouts de Paris](https://fr.wikipedia.org/wiki/%C3%89gouts_de_Paris))
- Belgrand's cleaning machines ([fr.wikipedia](https://fr.wikipedia.org/wiki/%C3%89gouts_de_Paris), [pariszigzag](https://www.pariszigzag.fr/sortir-paris/balade-paris/visite-des-egouts-de-paris/)):
  - bateau-vanne: a boat with a gate across the channel, held by chains, for the main collectors; a moving dam whose head of water scours the sand.
    A modern one is 10 m long, 2.10 m wide and weighs about five tonnes;
  - wagon-vanne: the same idea on wheels, resting on the side walkways of the narrower secondary collectors;
  - wagon-bi-boules (museum piece, out of use since 2005): cleared sand in collectors narrower than 2.20 m;
  - wooden balls (boules) sized to the pipe, pushed by the flow to clean the siphons under the Seine.
- Paris sewermen "in the old days" had no gloves, helmets or boots, says the museum's chief sewerman (no date given). ([pariszigzag](https://www.pariszigzag.fr/sortir-paris/balade-paris/visite-des-egouts-de-paris/))
- London flushermen, 1851: in low sewers they worked "on our bellies, like frogs, with a rake between our legs".
  Near brewhouses the steam was "a regular London fog". ([Mayhew, London Labour and the London Poor, vol. 2](https://dl.tufts.edu/teiviewer/parent/rv043431c/chapter/c8s32))
- Fauna in Paris sewers: brown rats, cockroaches, spiders, centipedes, fungi. ([fr.wikipedia](https://fr.wikipedia.org/wiki/%C3%89gouts_de_Paris))
